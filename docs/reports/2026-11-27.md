# Wikipedia Discussion Report

## AI-related discussions

### The big one: should the anti-AI writing guideline become binding policy?
**[Promote WP:Large language model (LLM) from guideline to policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AVillage_pump_%28policy%29/Promote_WP%3ALLM_from_guideline_to_policy#Promote_%5B%5BWP%3ALLM%5D%5D_from_guideline_to_policy)** (Village Pump, filed as a formal Request for Comment) — With 632 comments and 127 participants, this is by far the largest and most consequential discussion running right now. The guideline on writing articles with large language models (shortcut WP:LLM) is already being enforced like a rule — editors are blocked and administrators have even been desysopped (stripped of admin tools) for violating it — so the proposal is simply to call it what it functionally already is: a policy. Support is broad, but opponents argue this is symbolic housekeeping, or that a policy needs a clearer "permitted use" carve-out for editors who use large language models constructively. Given the size and the real enforcement stakes (policy status makes violations harder to argue away as "just a guideline"), this is worth close tracking as it nears a close.

### A practical flashpoint: browsers are now bundling AI writing assistants
**[Microsoft Edge just turned into a large-language-model-violating machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AVillage_pump_%28policy%29#Microsoft_Edge_just_turned_into_a_%5B%5BWP%3ANOLLM%5D%5D_violating_machine.)** (Village Pump, 81 comments) — An editor noticed Microsoft Edge now pops up an AI rewrite tool ("polish this draft," "make the tone more empathetic") whenever text is selected, raising fears that good-faith editors will unknowingly violate the no-large-language-model policy (shortcut WP:NOLLM) just by using a mainstream browser. The thread turns into a sharp debate over where the line sits between the "permitted" use of AI for spelling/grammar fixes and the "banned" use for substantive rewriting — several editors argue the carve-out for simple corrections is a loophole being exploited and should be narrowed or scrapped, while others (citing their own good experiences with grammar-checking tools) warn against a "ban everything" overreaction.

### Formalizing rules for AI agents, not just human AI users
**[RfC: AI agent policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AVillage_pump_%28policy%29#RfC%3A_AI_agent_policy)** (Village Pump, 68 comments, 42 participants, still getting comments within the last day) — A proposal to make the draft "AI agent policy" (covering autonomous AI agents that edit Wikipedia directly, as opposed to humans using AI tools) an official policy rather than just an essay. Support is near-unanimous so far; one notable argument is that large language models are trained to treat "policy" as more binding than "guideline" or "essay," so giving agents (and their operators) an unambiguous, clearly-labeled policy to point to is itself a practical enforcement mechanism. This pairs naturally with the LLM-guideline promotion debate above — together they show the community moving to tighten formal status around all its AI-related rules at once.

### Should Wikipedia build its own AI search assistant?
**[AI-assisted search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AVillage_pump_%28idea_lab%29#AI-assisted_search)** (Idea Lab, 86 comments, 211 AI mentions — the single most AI-saturated thread this round) — A proposal that the Wikimedia Foundation build an in-house, strictly Wikipedia-sourced AI search/summary tool to compete with chatbots that are (the proposer argues) drawing readers away from Wikipedia itself. It's pitched with heavy guardrails (freely-licensed software only, never replacing the encyclopedia text, no hallucination). Replies are skeptical: several ask how this differs from just telling any chatbot to restrict itself to Wikipedia sources, and whether the cost is justified. Worth watching as a sign of the community grappling with AI not just as a threat to content integrity but as a competitor for readership.

### A real-world dispute that captures how messy AI accusations get
**[Request for independent review of users' conduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AAdministrators%27_noticeboard#Request_for_independent_review_of_users_conduct)** (Administrators' noticeboard, 132 comments, the single most AI-saturated noticeboard thread this round) — An editor accused of undisclosed AI use asked for "independent review" of how two other editors handled the accusation against them — except the appeal itself reads as AI-generated, which other editors immediately pointed out, undermining the editor's claim they only use AI for "source discovery." It's a vivid illustration of how hard it now is to litigate AI-use accusations on talk pages, and how quickly such disputes spiral into meta-arguments about tone and assumption of good faith (shortcut WP:AGF).

### Enforcement outcomes and ongoing abuse cases
- **[Unblock for LLM misuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AAdministrators%27_noticeboard#Unblock_for_LLM_misuse)** (Administrators' noticeboard) — Already closed. A prolific, previously autopatrolled editor who was partially blocked for years of heavy, undisclosed large-language-model use asked for a second chance; while the appeal was pending, a checkuser caught them evading the block with a sock puppet account. Result: declined, indefinite block. A clear case study in how "I've reformed" appeals are landing right now.
- **[Interesting case of agentic or LLM-assisted abuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AAdministrators%27_noticeboard#Interesting_case_of_agentic_or_LLM-assisted_abuse_%28Update%3A_actually%2C_action_is_needed_now%29)** (Administrators' noticeboard) — Already resolved (accounts blocked and globally locked), but the writeup is a genuinely interesting technical forensics case: a sock-puppet network made rapid, parallel, multi-language edits inserting malicious links, with tell-tale signs of automation (API edits with no human-editor tags, synchronized sandbox "training" edits, templated fake user pages). The thread proposes new detection ideas for autonomous/agentic abuse going forward.
- **[LLM-generated reports in Middle East Monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AReliable_sources%2FNoticeboard#LLM-generated_reports_in_Middle_East_Monitor_%28MEMO%29)** (Reliable Sources Noticeboard, 35 comments) — Editors used AI-detection tools (Pangram, GPTZero) to show that a news outlet already under scrutiny for AI-generated propaganda op-eds is also publishing unbylined "news" reports that are mostly AI-generated, including one that fabricates a direct quote from Benjamin Netanyahu. Since the outlet is cited roughly 1,300 times across Wikipedia, a "generally unreliable" or deprecation finding here would trigger a large cleanup.
- **[Application of an AI tag for no reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AAdministrators%27_noticeboard/Incidents#Application_of_AI_tag_for_no_reason%2C_refusal_to_elaborate_on_article%27s_supposed_problems)** (Administrators' Noticeboard/Incidents, closed) — An article creator complained that another editor slapped an AI-generated-content maintenance tag on their work without explanation. On investigation, the tag turned out to be justified (red-linked categories apparently invented by an AI tool, a miscited source, use of an AI chatbot to do unsourced currency conversion); the complainant was warned that "assume good faith" cuts both ways.
- **[Prof. JS2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AAdministrators%27_noticeboard/Incidents#Prof._JS2)** (Administrators' Noticeboard/Incidents) — A conduct complaint alleging an editor uses ChatGPT to find sources on ethnicity/genetics topics but never actually reads them, leading to repeated misrepresentation of citations and edit-warring; a topic ban from Iran/Arab-related subjects has been proposed.
- **[Carrot juice, aspersions, and AGF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AAdministrators%27_noticeboard/Incidents#Carrot_juice%2C_aspersions%2C_and_AGF)** (Administrators' Noticeboard/Incidents, 66 comments, very active) — A dispute over the "plant intelligence" article where, among other assume-good-faith violations, one editor is accused of baselessly accusing another of using a large language model or AI chatbot to "quote-mine" sources — another example of AI-use accusations being deployed as a rhetorical weapon in content disputes rather than as genuine detection.
- **[Targeting, canvassing and likely meatpuppetry on Nigerian Yoruba subjects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AAdministrators%27_noticeboard/Incidents#Targeting%2C_canvassing_and_likely_meatpuppetry_by_certain_users_on_Nigerian_Yoruba_subjects)** (Administrators' Noticeboard/Incidents, very fresh, last comment under an hour ago) — A sprawling, heated ethnic-topic dispute where several accused editors have had their Articles-for-deletion votes and comments hatted (collapsed) as AI-generated; a sockpuppet investigation has been requested. Messy and still unfolding, but notable as another instance where AI-generated contributions are allegedly being used to manipulate deletion discussions.

**Pattern worth noting for the book:** across today's reports, "AI use" has become as much a social/rhetorical weapon in editor disputes (accusing an opponent of secretly using a chatbot) as it is a genuine content-integrity problem (actual AI-fabricated citations, hallucinated categories, or propaganda). The community is simultaneously trying to tighten formal policy (the WP:LLM-to-policy and AI-agent-policy RfCs) while individual noticeboard cases show how uneven and contentious enforcement still is on the ground.
